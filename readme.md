# [Resource Allocation] Power Allocation Strategy for Target Localization in a Distributed Multi-Radar Architecture [with MATLAB Code]

1. Abstract: The distributed multi-radar architecture has significant advantages in target positioning parameter estimation, effectively enhancing estimation performance. When there are many radars, the minimum estimation mean square error (MSE) achieved by fully resource allocation may exceed the preset system performance goals. In view of this, this paper proposes a performance-driven resource allocation scheme for multi-radar systems, which will utilize all available antennas during the positioning process.
Under the premise of meeting the preset estimation MSE threshold, this scheme aims to minimize total transmission energy while ensuring that the transmission power of each radar station is within an acceptable range. Given a total power budget, it optimizes the power allocation between the transmitting radars to achieve the minimization of positioning MSE.
The Cramer-Rao bound (CRB) is used as the optimization metric for estimation MSE. For the resulting non-convex optimization problem, the relaxation method and domain decomposition method are employed for solving, which are both suitable for centralized processing at the fusion center and distributed processing.
Research results show that uniform power allocation is not the optimal solution. The proposed power allocation algorithm can find local optima, achieving better positioning MSE under the same power budget; or achieving the same positioning MSE performance with lower required power. Additionally, the above conclusions are also physically explained in this paper.

Introduction (I) Background and Motivation: In recent years, the use of multiple widely distributed site radar architectures has gradually emerged, such as multi-input multi-output (MIMO) radar systems and multi-base station radar systems with widely distributed antennas. Compared to single-antenna radars (i.e., single-base station radars), these systems have shown significant advantages.
The MIMO radar system with widely distributed antennas leverages the advantages of larger spatial distribution to enhance target positioning capabilities. Research has analyzed the performance of positioning estimation in terms of Cramer-Rao bound (CRB) and found that its improvement is directly proportional to the product of the number of transmitting and receiving antennas. Additionally, an analysis of the positioning performance of multi-base station radar systems reveals that the mean square error is a function of the geometric distribution. In MIMO radar systems employing non-coherent processing, the positioning mean square error is inversely proportional to the signal effective bandwidth, signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), and the product of the number of transmitting and receiving antennas.
Increasing the number of radars participating in positioning or enhancing the transmitted power can improve positioning performance. However, in practical applications, most systems have preset performance targets, including but not limited to, target positioning accuracy and maximum total radiated energy. Therefore, comprehensive utilization of system resources could lead to resource wastage, such as transmitter power and communication link resources between the radar and the central integration center.
Resource awareness design is crucial in radar applications. For example, for radars deployed on mobile sites or systems requiring long-term operation, cost optimization through resource awareness design can be achieved. For surveillance radars installed on vehicles with limited energy, and anti-missile defense radar systems relying on diesel generators for off-grid power supply, power awareness design helps extend the operational time before refueling. Moreover, in military operations in hostile environments, achieving low interception probability (LPI) operation may require minimizing the required transmitter power for task completion.
The target positioning in multi-radar systems essentially relies on multi-range measurements. Multi-range measurement refers to the calculation of target positions by measuring the distances to multiple reference positions, and in two-dimensional space, at least three range measurements are required to determine the target's position. The feasible positioning accuracy of multi-range measurement is not only determined by the geographical distribution of radar deployment but also related to the effective bandwidth and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Among these, the SNR comprehensively considers path loss, target radar cross section (RCS), and transmitting power. Existing research has demonstrated that the mean square error of positioning estimation is associated with the radar system distribution.
In previous studies, a fixed system power budget was uniformly allocated to each transmitting radar. So, when it is possible to pre-estimate the target radar cross section (which is more common in target tracking or multiple estimation periods), is uniform allocation still the best strategy? This paper explores this issue and proposes an optimal power allocation algorithm. The optimization problem is ultimately transformed into an non-convex, nonlinear optimization problem. One approach to solving such problems is to appropriately relax the original problem, then find the local minimum using the optimal solution; another method involves leveraging Lagrange multipliers, Kuhn-Tucker conditions, and decomposition techniques. This paper will apply both relaxation methods and domain decomposition methods to solve this optimization problem.
(II) Main Contributions:
1. Based on the Cramer-Rao bounds, we derive a power allocation algorithm. We clarify two types of power allocation optimization problems: the first type is minimizing total transmitting power given a certain precision requirement; the second type is maximizing achievable positioning accuracy given a power budget. By applying relaxation methods and decomposition methods, we derive centralized and distributed algorithms respectively.
2. Using domain decomposition methods, we obtain a set of analytical solutions. By constructing Kuhn-Tucker conditions for non-convex optimization problems, we derive a set of parallelizable and overlapping optimization subproblems. These subproblems can be solved in a distributed manner. Subsequently, we use the feasible solution set of the subproblems to derive the optimal power allocation scheme for the main problem, which is also applicable to both types of power allocation schemes.
3.
Numerical analysis results show that, compared to uniform power allocation, the proposed algorithm can achieve up to 50% total transmitter power savings; under a given total power budget, the positioning accuracy can reach twice that of the uniform allocation scheme.

The physical interpretation of the power allocation strategy obtained is helpful in understanding the intrinsic mechanism and rationality of power allocation.
III. Conclusion This paper proposes two power allocation schemes for resource-aware design of target localization in distributed multi-radar systems. One scheme aims to minimize total radiated power to achieve a preset positioning mean squared error threshold; the other scheme aims to minimize the achievable positioning mean squared error under a given total energy budget.
To characterize the positioning mean squared error, this paper adopts the closed-form expression of the Cramer-Rao bound. For the resulting non-convex optimization problem of power allocation, the Lagrange multiplier method and the Kuhn-Tucker conditions are used in conjunction with relaxation methods and domain decomposition methods for solving. Among them, the domain decomposition method generates a set of parallel and non-overlapping optimization subproblems suitable for distributed processing.
Research results indicate that uniform power allocation is not optimal. Through the proposed power allocation strategy, significant power savings or positioning accuracy improvements can be achieved. The optimal power allocation depends on the geometric distribution of the radar relative to the target's position and the radar cross-section of the target.
